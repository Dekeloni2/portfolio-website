Minecraft Level & World Design

I taught myself design here, years before college. From 2014 to 2025 I designed Minecraft servers: the worlds, the economies and the progression. This page covers six of them, across RPG, PvP and educational servers. Several ran live betas, one peaked at about 300 concurrent players, and a studio acquired one of my concepts.

Key decisions

  1. Split worlds into closed zones with level gates, so players discover the map in order instead of all at once. More ↓
  2. Gave every region its own look, so hundreds of players could find their way without a minimap. More ↓
  3. Tied difficulty to distance, so walking deeper into the world is the difficulty curve. More ↓
  4. Next time: scope to the team I have, and prove the core loop before building the whole world. More ↓
The problem

A single-player level can be linear. A server has hundreds of players wandering at once, and each of them needs to know where they are, where to go next and why, with no loading screen to hide the seams. These are the rules I ended up working by.

Zones and gates

The world is split into closed regions with few exits and level-gated entry, so players discover the map in order instead of all at once.

Landmarks

Every city and region has its own theme, colors and silhouette: a green farming valley, a trade port, a crime district full of fog. Players know where they are without a minimap.

Difficulty by distance

Enemy levels and rewards go up the deeper or farther a zone is. Walking through the world is the difficulty curve.

Pacing with paths

Paths, hubs and where I put NPCs and quests pull players forward and alternate tension with rest, so the level guides them instead of a quest marker.

The servers

Roughly ordered by how far they got. Some ran live. Others never launched and exist as design docs, so for those the design is what I'm showing, not a build.

Solo RPG: a world in closed zones

Designed · Full GDD
MMORPG-sized server I designed alone
What it is

An RPG server with branching progression (professions, collections, your own home) on top of a large world to explore.

The world

Closed ~70×70 zones, each with up to four exits. Cities set the level ranges: a starter valley in green mountains (lv 1–30), a trade port where every ship docks, a warrior village in chalk mountains, Arcanum, a magic city you have to earn your way into, a foggy crime district with a villain hiding in an underground railway, a graveyard city, and a forbidden Temple (lv 50–80). Midas' Tunnel goes down three floors, and danger and loot both rise on the way.

Systems: professions, collections that complete into stat-granting card sets, prestige and rebirth, a rune-peeling inventory minigame, and a parkour dimension for progressing without combat. Status: shelved for lack of time. I'd like to come back to it.

PixelStory: an RPG built on races

Closed beta · ~50 players
RPG server · core idea, balance, economy, all custom textures
What it is

Five playable races that fight differently. Elves use bows, giants are slow and tanky, fairies fly and support, dwarves are fast backstabbers, and angels unlock late.

The world

Forests, sci-fi mountain ranges, abandoned cities, a hidden underground city, and a huge graveyard built around one enormous tree. Each race has a home city, and the human capital is the main landmark. You can read gear at a glance: the stronger it is, the more detailed its texture.

My part: the core idea, balance and economy, and all of the custom textures (since lost). Outcome: about 50 players in closed beta. It closed when we ran out of money for a programmer.

School Planet: a space RPG that teaches English

Designed · Original concept
Educational RPG
What it is

Players explore a world in orbit as astronauts and aliens and pick up English by playing instead of studying.

Why I like it

The goal was learning, not only fun. Vocabulary and practice turn into quests and discoveries, so getting further into the world means getting further into the language. It's the design I'm proudest of attempting.

Status: fully designed. It started on the whiteboard in the photo. Development paused for a long time and fell behind technically, so I moved on to other projects.

Why so many didn't finish

Most of these closed for reasons outside the design: the money for a programmer ran out, teammates were drafted into the army, or my own free time ran out on a solo project. That's normal for volunteer Minecraft servers. The ones that did run (a ~50-player beta, a server that peaked near 300, a concept a studio bought) are where the design got tested by real players.

What I'd do differently: scope to what a small volunteer team can actually ship, and prove the core loop before building the whole world.
