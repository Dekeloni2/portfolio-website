Tower of Games

A deckbuilder where the deck is board game pieces. You walk a branching tower map in 2.5D, and fights play out on a grid where pieces move and act on your command. The thing that is your life is a piece standing on that grid — and climbing means reaching the one standing on theirs.

EngineUnreal Engine 5, Blueprints
GenreDeckbuilder + grid tactics
PlatformPC
ContextTiltan College, Introduction to Unreal
TeamSolo: design and implementation
StatusPrototype — grid, movement and cards driving movement in engine

Key decisions

  1. I abandoned the design question I started from, because I couldn't answer it and everything built on it would have been arbitrary. More ↓
  2. I reversed onto a playable grid after explicitly ruling one out. More ↓
  3. Every commander stands on the board — but only one of them moves. More ↓
  4. Drawing a card costs your whole turn, which only works once there's something worth doing instead. More ↓
The question I couldn't answer

I started from one question: if cards had never existed in real life, what piece would replace them? I wanted to break the rules a card takes for granted — that it's placed down to be played, that it carries an ability, that it sometimes has a cost. The first answer I liked was a deck built from board game pieces, with a class made mostly of chess pieces, each with health, damage and an effect.

I set the question aside. I couldn't answer it beyond the chess idea, and by then I had also ruled out a playable board — and without a board, the chess framing collapses. Keeping a question I couldn't answer at the centre would have made every later decision arbitrary. The pieces survived; the thesis didn't. Chess became one class's set rather than the whole premise, which is also why the game spans board games generally instead of one.

Reversing onto the board

The settled shape was 2.5D, a map walked with WASD, and a fixed arena camera for combat in the style of Slay the Spire. No playable board. That left fights as a card game with no spatial layer, in a game whose cards are board game pieces.

I reversed it. Combat is now a grid in the vein of Duelyst: pieces occupy tiles and move where I send them. The map stayed Slay the Spire's branching structure, but the lines are corridors you walk rather than nodes you click, and you can walk back at a branch.

What the design is built on

Your life is a piece

No abstract health anywhere. Both sides have a commander standing on the grid. Winning means reaching theirs; losing means losing yours.

Keywords before content

Set, Act, Choose and Extend are the vocabulary. New pieces are combinations of keywords, not new rules.

Nothing is hidden

Enemies telegraph everything: where they'll move, who they'll buff, the card they intend to play. The difficulty is in reading it, not in guessing.

A turn is one choice

Each piece does one of move, defend or attack. Drawing costs the whole turn. Everything competes for the same scarce thing.

The decision I'm proudest of: commanders on the board

This is the change that made the grid matter, and it started as a complaint about my own design.

The problem

The game is named after boards, but almost nothing about the grid mattered. Enemy health sat off the board behind their back row. Killing enemy pieces wasn't the win condition. Defend couldn't protect allies. So enemy pieces weren't obstacles to clear and couldn't shield anything — my pieces walked toward a wall to poke an abstract number. Strip the art away and the grid was a range meter.

The fix I rejected first

CONSIDERED · every commander identical on the boardBUILT · same board, different behaviour

Putting all commanders on the board as the same kind of piece cleans up the rules, but it costs the Architect his identity: if everyone's life is a piece, "my life is a piece" stops being his class and becomes the rulebook. That trade was too expensive, so I kept the idea and changed the shape.

What I built instead

BEFORE · two health modelsAFTER · one model, a can act flag

The default commander is a passive objective: it has health, holds a tile, can be shielded, and does nothing. The Architect's King is the exception — his commander plays. It moves and takes actions like any piece.

Plays

  • The Architect's King
  • Moves and acts like any piece
  • Can be walked out of danger
  • Or walked into a fight it shouldn't take

Sits

  • Every regular enemy and Gatekeeper
  • An objective to reach and kill
  • Their pieces body-block it
  • Defend can shield it

Sits and decays

  • The Arcanist — the wizard himself
  • Immobile, no placement phase
  • His pressure comes from pieces that fade
  • Each fade hands him Armor

Why this was worth reversing a settled design

It gave the board a front that advances, so position, range and tempo all became live at once. It made the Architect's two existing costs make sense — the placement phase exists because a commander that acts needs positioning up front, and it eats one of his three capacity slots because it's genuinely a unit, not a health bar. And it answered an open question I'd been stuck on for days.

It was also less work than what it replaced: it deleted the abstract-health path, the off-board health UI and the branch in damage resolution that handled two models, in exchange for one commander class with a flag. The final boss stopped being a special case too — he was the only fight where the design had been fully itself, which in hindsight was the tell.

Drawing costs a turn

There's no free draw at the start of a turn. You open a fight with three cards, and after that pressing Draw ends your turn. Your hand isn't discarded, it persists, and it caps at five.

For a while this was broken and I knew it without being able to say why. If drawing costs a turn, something else has to be worth that turn — and for the Architect, nothing was. Once his board was set and his King was walled behind it, there was no reason not to draw forever. The button was a dead choice dressed up as a decision.

Putting the enemy commander on the board fixed it as a side effect. A reachable objective creates a race: a turn you spend drawing is a turn you don't advance, while your opponent does. Drawing became a tempo decision, which is what I wanted it to be in the first place.

What this taught me

I'd been treating the draw economy as a numbers problem — hand size, costs, how many cards per draw. It wasn't. It was a missing source of pressure somewhere else entirely in the game, and no amount of tuning the draw itself would have found it.

Keywords

The keyword system is the first thing I wanted to build, before I'd reasoned my way to needing it. Four are defined and two slots are deliberately open, so pieces I design later can want something I haven't invented yet.

Set

The piece is dormant the turn it's deployed. When its condition triggers, the bonus fires and it can act normally after that. Setting is that piece's turn.

Act

An on-deploy trigger. The effect fires the moment the piece hits the board. Summoned pieces never trigger it, so summon chains can't feed themselves.

Choose

Presents two or three options affecting the board or the piece. The decision point sits inside the piece rather than before it.

Extend

Hits an enemy commander from anywhere, ignoring range. This is the anti-turtle keyword: it's why walling up a commander isn't a strategy.

Defend didn't survive as a keyword. It became an action instead, alongside move and attack, because it isn't a property a piece has — it's a thing a piece spends its turn doing.

A worked example — the Rook

"Set: your King gains +10 Armor for 2 turns. Act: if your King is at 50% HP or below, summon 1 additional Rook." Two keywords, no new rules, and a piece that reads differently depending on how badly the fight is going.

Where it stands

It's early. The prototype has the grid, camera control, piece movement and cards driving that movement. Combat, keywords and the tower are ahead of it.

Settled

  • Commanders on the board, with one model and a can-act flag
  • Grid combat with pieces that move on command
  • Four keywords, two slots held open on purpose
  • Draw as a turn cost rather than a free top-up
  • Fully telegraphed enemies — no hidden information
  • Defend as an action, not a keyword

Still open

  • Commander distance. Too close and a fight is a two-turn rush; too far behind too many bodies and clearing the board is the only strategy. This is the next thing I test.
  • The two remaining keyword slots
  • The opening draw-and-shuffle rule
  • Whether the Arcanist needs a capacity bonus to stay viable

What I'd change next

I designed the keyword vocabulary before I had a board worth using it on, and the draw economy before there was pressure to make drawing a real choice. Both ended up fine, but both were fixed by changing something else entirely — which tells me I was designing the pieces before the space they sit in. Next time the board comes first.
