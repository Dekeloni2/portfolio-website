TiltanTale

An Undertale-inspired RPG built in MonoGame. You play as a student who has fallen into Tiltan College of Visual Design and Communication and must navigate the school, face your teachers as bosses, and find a way back to the overworld. Features overworld exploration and a turn-based bullet hell battle system — attack, act, use items, or spare enemies once the right conditions are met.

Design Pillars
01

The Fight Is a Conversation

Every encounter can end in violence or mercy. FIGHT, ACT, ITEM, SPARE — the player decides whether a teacher is an obstacle or a person to understand. Combat carries narrative weight.

02

Readable, Fair Bullet Hell

Attack patterns are tough but honest — telegraphed, learnable, beatable on skill. Difficulty comes from reading the pattern, not from cheap hits. Each boss escalates as the fight goes on.

03

Teachers as Bosses

Each boss is a Tiltan College teacher, and their fight reflects who they are — their subject, their personality, their quirks are baked into their attack patterns and dialogue. The school itself is the antagonist and the joke.

04

Personality in Every Frame

Undertale earned its love through character and tone. TiltanTale leans into that — writing, expressions, and pattern design all serve the humor and heart of being a student trying to escape their own design school.

Battle System

Combat alternates between two phases. On your turn you choose an action from the menu; on the enemy's turn, control shifts to your CLOVER — a token you steer through a bullet-hell attack inside the battle box. Survive the pattern, and it's your turn again.

1
Choose FIGHT · ACT · ITEM · MERCY
2
Enemy turn — dodge the bullet pattern with your CLOVER
3
Read the tell, weave through, take no hits
4
Wear them down — or meet the SPARE condition and end it peacefully
Core Actions

FIGHT

A timing-based strike: a marker sweeps across the attack bar — land it dead center for max damage. The straightforward path, but fighting every teacher isn't always the ending you want.

ACT

Talk, observe, or interact in boss-specific ways. The right ACTs change the fight, soften a teacher, and open the path to sparing them.

ITEM

Heal and use consumables collected through the school. Resource management matters when a boss has a long, escalating pattern set.

MERCY

Spare an enemy once its condition is met. Ending a fight without a knockout is its own kind of win — and shapes how the school responds to you.

The Battle System — In Motion

The battle system is TiltanTale's deepest and most polished layer — a full turn-based bullet-hell built on MonoGame, where every system is authored in code with no engine-level tooling doing the heavy lifting. No visual editor placed these patterns, no animation timeline drives the teachers — it's all hand-built.

It's designed as a scalable framework, not a set of one-off fights: new teachers — with their own patterns, animations, and spare conditions — can be built on top of it with relative ease.

This was my first time designing bosses from the ground up — patterns, pacing, difficulty curves, personality, and the systems tying them together. Going from no boss-design experience to a scalable framework with 15+ hand-built encounters is the piece of work I've grown the most on.

Game Feel & Character

Every pattern is built from a real trait of the teacher it belongs to — an in-joke, a quirk, a thing they always say. It's the "personality drives design" pillar taken literally: the attack is the character. All readable, escalating, and rendered from code.

These are just a taste — each teacher has many more. Over 15 custom attack patterns were built for the game. Play it to find them all.

Your Choices Matter — Spare vs. Defeat

The same teacher, two endings — and the visual language carries the moral weight. Spare them and they resolve peacefully, dissolving into a harmless puff of smoke; they still exist in the world afterward. Defeat them and it's a brutal end — final words, then they crumble to dust, gone for good. Smoke means they're still out there; dust means you erased them. The core pillar, told through how a character leaves the screen.

The point isn't just a branching outcome — it's that the game hands you the power and refuses to tell you what to do with it. Every teacher is someone you could understand instead of destroy, and mercy is always harder than the fight. Whether you take it says something about you, not the game. That discomfort is the design goal: a choice the player has to own.

Combat Systems — The Attack Mini-Game

Under the feel is a real, readable system: the timing mini-game on FIGHT, live HP updating and display, and the strike animation resolving. Systems, not scripted one-offs.

Narrative Design

TiltanTale is built around a story, not just a battle system. You play a student who has fallen into Tiltan and must fight or befriend their way out — a premise that turns the school itself into the world you navigate. Spare and fight choices open different dialogue routes and lead to multiple endings; no single playthrough sees everything.

01

Premise & Hook

A student trapped in their own design school, trying to escape. Relatable hook, absurd situation — grounded enough to care about, strange enough to be funny. The escape goal gives every room and boss a clear narrative purpose.

02

Choice-Driven Story

Because fights can end in a knockout or a spared enemy, the player's choices shape how the school responds to them and how the story reads. The narrative and the combat system aren't separate layers — the way you fight is the way you tell the story.

03

Character Through Combat

Each teacher-boss reveals who they are through dialogue and how they fight. Personality and quirks are expressed in-battle — the fight itself teaches you who this person is.

04

Tone: Humor with Heart

The writing balances comedy about student life at Tiltan with genuine emotional stakes about wanting out. Holding that tonal line — funny without being hollow — is the core narrative-design challenge, and it's what makes the escape land.

Endings & Consequence

Every fight ends in mercy or a knockout, and the game remembers all of them. What decides your epilogue isn't how many teachers you spared — it's which ones. Every possible combination has its own ending, written from scratch. None of them are variations on a shared template, and no two share an emotional register. What the game has to say about your run depends entirely on who is still standing at the end of it.

01

No Placeholder Endings

The easy version of this system is one generic "neutral" epilogue with names swapped in. TiltanTale doesn't do that. Every combination is authored separately, with its own argument about what the player did — because an ending that could belong to any run doesn't say anything about yours.

02

The Cast Reconfigures

Survivors don't simply grieve — they change. Losing a particular person shifts how the remaining cast behaves, what they believe, and which of them ends up carrying a role nobody else is left to fill. Who is missing reshapes who the others become.

03

Every Ending Is Its Own Timeline

No epilogue winks at another. Nothing references events the player didn't live through in that specific run, and no character knows anything they couldn't have learned in the world you actually made. Each ending is internally airtight — the coherence is the respect.

04

It Never Tells You If You Were Right

No ending grades the player. There's no score, no reveal that a route was the "true" one, no voice explaining the moral. The people left behind react honestly, and the player is left to decide what that means. The discomfort is the design goal.

Eight endings. One playthrough sees exactly one of them.

Seeing the whole picture means going back and making choices you may not want to make — which is the entire point. If you play it, play it more than once. The endings you have to work hardest to reach are the ones the game has the most to say about.

Map & Level Design

Built by hand to realize the school as a navigable space. Level design here is about readability and pacing — guiding the player, placing encounters where they land, and letting the environment tell part of the story.

Playtesting & Iteration

Late in development I sat a friend down for a full playtest and watched without steering — one session, real-time notes. Then I triaged the feedback: implement what served the game, defer what didn't. The decision of what not to build mattered as much as the fixes.

Cut attack durations — measured against the genre

BEFORE · final ≈ 60s AFTER · trimmed to feel

The playtest flagged that fights dragged. Undertale's longest attacks top out near 40 seconds — yet my basic attacks were hitting that ceiling and the Cloverbyte final ran close to a full minute. If a basic attack costs as much player attention as a boss's climax, the pacing is broken. I shortened both the Yakir final-lesson and Cloverbyte patterns; the finales now read as finales instead of endurance tests.

Removed back-to-back attack repeats

BEFORE · same attack could repeat AFTER · no consecutive repeats

David and Dor could fire the same attack twice in a row. Players parse boss fights as patterns, and repetition flattens tension and telegraphs the wrong things. I added a rule so these characters can't repeat an attack consecutively — keeping each exchange readable and varied.

Both changes came from the same instinct: a boss fight is a rhythm, and the player has to be able to read it. Length and variety are the levers.

Same session — UX & quality-of-life pass

The playtest also surfaced a batch of friction points I cleared in one pass:

Long-hold to quit (1.25s)
Escape used to close instantly — one misclick and you're out. Exiting is now deliberate.
In-game settings (C menu)
Settings were external-only; now adjustable on the fly from the menu.
Split SFX / BGM
One volume slider became separate sound-effect and music controls.
Fullscreen (F11)
Added a proper fullscreen toggle.
Hall music mix
The Tiltan Hall track sat too loud against everything else — lowered to sit right.

Scoped out: the playtester suggested in-game signs could pull live photos from Tiltan. I deferred it — signs will carry small jokes and flavor rather than a system that adds complexity without proportional payoff. Knowing when a good idea isn't worth the build is part of the job.
