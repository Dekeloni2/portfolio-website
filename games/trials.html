Trials of the Dungeon

A cooperative board game for 1–4 players. Navigate a randomly generated dungeon, gear up, and grow strong enough to defeat the final boss — The Slime King. Designed and produced as a physical product for the Game Design course.

Design Pillars
01

Cooperative Tension

One win condition, one lose condition. Classes cover each other's weaknesses — the Slime King punishes parties that didn't coordinate.

02

Class Identity

Each class plays differently by design. Warrior tanks, Healer sustains, Witch deals spell damage, Paladin balances both. Your class shapes how you approach every encounter.

03

Emergent Exploration

The dungeon is built from a shuffled tile deck drawn during play. No two runs produce the same layout — each session is fresh without a single pre-designed level.

04

Meaningful Progression

Gear, XP, and gold all serve one goal: survive The Slime King. Every decision is framed against that — pacing the economy was the hardest design problem.

Key Systems

Tile-Based Exploration

Players draw and place tiles each turn — Monster, Treasure, Shop, Trap, or Blacksmith. The dungeon grows as you explore; mandatory reveals keep the threat alive.

Combat with Guard & Crit

Monsters have a Guard range (minimum roll to deal damage) and a Crit range (roll for 2× damage). Even standard encounters carry real tension — not just "roll high, win."

Shop & Blacksmith Economy

Gold buys spells from the Shopkeeper and upgrades from the Blacksmith. Equipment degrades and must be repaired — a resource loop that runs the entire session.

The Slime King

The pacing forcing function — everything in the game is designed around whether the party is ready. Unique abilities not seen in regular encounters reward intentional builds.

Design Note

The only physical product in this portfolio. Tabletop design means no engine to enforce rules, no UI to communicate state — everything has to work through the components alone. The economy runs on two gold sinks (Shopkeeper for consumables, Blacksmith for upgrades) tuned for constant tension between saving and spending. Writing the rulebook was its own challenge: unambiguous rules that cover every edge case without overwhelming new players. Shipped as a complete, playable product.

Post Mortem

Judge Feedback & Reflection

Trials of the Dungeon was presented to a panel of judges. Here's what they flagged, what they praised, and what I'd do differently.

What Worked

  • Combat was praised for its simplicity — roll a die, resolve the outcome. No overcomplicated systems blocking the fun.
  • Progression landed well — players felt real growth without it feeling arbitrary.
  • Clear win condition. Players always know their goal: get strong enough to defeat The Slime King.

What Didn't / Would Change

  • The tile deck and monster pool should have been seeded. Fully shuffled, players could hit a powerful monster in the first room. Controlling encounter order would make progression far more consistent.
  • Spell costs and monster HP were too high. Balance was the weakest part — it needed more playtesting.
  • Spell text created friction. Custom icons would've dramatically improved readability — board game players don't want to read paragraphs mid-play.

Key Takeaway

Physical design punishes imbalance more than digital — there's no patch, no hotfix. These issues only surface through playtesting with real people, and we didn't do enough of it. The lesson: playtesting isn't a final step — it is the design process.
