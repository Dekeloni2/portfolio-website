The Merge Rule — The Whole System on One Idea

Every craft in the game is exactly two items in, one item out — same type or different. There is no crafting bench, no furnace, no upgrade menu. Even ore becomes an ingot by merging two ore. The constraint is the design: one rule the player learns in the first ten seconds, then never has to relearn.

The interesting problem is making that one rule carry 117 items across five tiers without turning into a memorisation test. It does that through three layered rules below.

01 · Tier-up is uniform

You never re-craft a finished item from scratch. Merge it with that tier's catalyst and it becomes the same item, one tier up. Two catalyst tracks keep weapons and armour on separate material economies, so a good weapon run doesn't automatically hand you good armour.

Armour & Tools — structural
Stone Chunk→Iron Ingot→Refined Crystal→Voidsteel
Weapons — ore ingots
Copper→Iron→Silver→Hell
All seven ores earn their place: Copper, Iron, Silver and Hell are the weapon tier catalysts. Gold is the economy metal — it brews the Fortune Draught (+30% survival points), tying mining to the score economy instead of to combat. Moon is Expert-only and forges the apex Lunar weapons. No ore is decorative.

02 · Difficulty gates the catalysts

Materials don't sit around waiting. Each catalyst only becomes reachable in a later difficulty phase, and phase is tied to how long you've survived. That's what turns difficulty from a pure cost into an opportunity — the only path to T5 runs straight through the hardest part of the run.

Easy
Baseline drops
Branch, Stone, Flower, Vine, Zombie Steak. Full T1 gear, low drop rates.
Normal
Uncommon unlocks
+ Zombie Tooth, Quartz, Copper & Iron ore. The iron line opens → T2/T3 gear.
Hard
Rare tier
+ Corrupted Claw, Infected Blood, Gem, Silver & Gold ore. Refined Crystal → T4 gear.
Expert
Endgame
+ Dense Bone, Zombie Core, Diamond, Moon & Hell ore. Voidsteel → T5 and chase items.
Why this matters: difficulty is usually a pure cost. Here it's also the loot table. Pushing into Expert means tougher enemies and the only access to T5 materials — so "how long do I stay?" becomes the run's central decision.

03 · Flat early, percentage late

T1–T3 gear grants flat stats so early upgrades feel punchy and readable. T4–T5 switch to percentages so late gear multiplies cleanly instead of double-dipping on flat bonuses — and so it would stack with the shop's percentage tier (a system designed, then cut for scope). It's a small rule that keeps the endgame from either flattening out or exploding.

Shop Economy — Designed, Cut for Scope
Designed, then cut. This system was fully specced but deliberately cut from this build's scope — included here to show the intended economy design and the decision behind cutting it.

Survival points would buy two kinds of upgrade — flat stats first, percentage multipliers once the flats are in place. The ordering is deliberate: percentages are worthless until there's something to multiply, so the shop teaches its own build order.

Flat Upgrades
+ Max HP
+ Attack
+ Defense
+ Crit Chance
% Multipliers
+% Attack
+% Max HP
+% Defense
+% Crit Damage & Move Speed
Run-only by design: stats reset each session. Every run is a fresh economy — no permanent power creep, and no guarantee you'll reach the shop again.
A crafting gateway too: the shop sells rare materials that can't be reliably farmed, wiring the point economy directly into the crafting tree.
Weapons — Three Archetypes

Each line trades attack speed against per-hit damage and all three land in the same DPS band per tier — the choice is feel, not power. The Dagger is the crit archetype and carries the game's only weapon bonus; Club and Spear stay clean damage-and-speed profiles.

Beyond T5 sits an optional apex: a finished Void weapon plus a Moon Ingot forges its Lunar capstone — the strongest weapons in the game and the real endgame chase.

Damage scales to the HP curve: per-hit damage grows roughly 3–4× per tier, taking DPS from ~13 at T1 to ~1,760 at T5 and ~3,040 at apex — then armour percentages (and the shop's, had it shipped) stack on top. Late-phase enemies run into the thousands of HP, so the curve is built to chase them rather than flatten out.
On the numbers themselves: these are a deliberate first pass, tuned for feel over balance on a five-day ship — the shape of the curve matters more here than any individual value, and the game is meant to feel generous rather than finely balanced. That's a survivable trade-off because every number lives in the data table, not in code: the entire curve can be re-tuned from the spreadsheet and re-exported without touching the game.
Armour — Six Slots, Two Rarities

Four common slots run the full T1–T5 ladder and lean defensive. Two rare slots — Gloves and Shoulders — are unavailable at T1 and always need a rarer base, and they carry the offensive stats. Stats cross from flat to percentage at T4, the same point the design's percentage layer kicks in.

Design note — Gloves & Shoulders: inspired by MapleStory's rarity curve. Void Shoulders (+6% ALL stats) is intentionally the hardest item in the game — it needs Voidsteel, whose own recipe pulls in a Zombie Core, so finishing it means repeatedly surviving into Expert.
Materials — The Five-Tier Ladder

Every chain starts with ground pickups and climbs through same-material refinements. Two cross-line merges open the tier gates: Refined Crystal (Quartz Cluster + Gem) and Void Shard (Zombie Core + Diamond) — tying progression directly to the drop table.

Separate ladders, on purpose: cordage (Vine → Cord → Rope) and alchemy (Flower → Crushed Petals → Flower Essence) are kept apart so binding material never distils into essence. Small consistency rules like this are what stop a 117-item tree from feeling arbitrary.
Design in Practice — Reworking a Dead-End Material

The tree didn't arrive in its current shape. This is the clearest example of a pass that changed it — auditing every material to check it actually fed something, and finding one that didn't.

Before — Gold was a dead end

  • Seven ore types existed as art assets, so the system was built to use all seven.
  • Six had a clear job: Copper, Iron, Silver and Hell as the weapon tier catalysts, Moon as the apex material.
  • Gold refined into a Gold Ingot that nothing consumed — a leaf node on the graph.
  • Players would mine it, read it as rare, and find no use. That reads as an oversight, not a choice.

After — Gold became the economy metal

  • Gold Ingot + Flower Essence → Fortune Draught, +30% survival points earned for 25s.
  • Gold now connects mining to the point economy instead of to combat — the one pillar no other ore touched.
  • Every ore now has a distinct role, and none of them overlap.
  • The graph has no orphan nodes: every material feeds at least one recipe.

Why not just make a Gold weapon?

That was the obvious fix and it was the wrong one. A fifth weapon catalyst would have meant a fifth tier-up track, which breaks the uniform tier-up rule the whole system rests on — and it would have made Gold redundant with Silver rather than distinct from it. Pointing it at the point economy instead gave it a job nothing else had, without adding a rule.

The same pass cut Sickle, Obsidian and Bronze for the opposite reason: they existed, but nothing consumed them and no gameplay needed them. Verifying the graph first confirmed tools and weapons are dead ends by design, so removing them cost nothing downstream. The working rule that came out of it: if a material doesn't feed something, it's decoration — give it a purpose or cut it.

Design Focus & Open Problems
01

Every merge has to feel like progress

The hardest problem in the system, and not fully solved. The uniform tier-up helps — each merge visibly changes the item's name and tier colour rather than a hidden stat — but mid-ladder merges still need a stronger flourish to read as a win.

02

Tuning the point economy

Points have to accumulate fast enough to feel rewarding and slow enough that spending them — in the shop the design calls for — feels earned. Because everything resets each run, this curve decides whether a session feels generous or pointless.

03

Gate by supply, not by steps

Tier-up is deliberately trivial to perform and hard to supply. Progression speed is tuned through drop rates on Quartz, Gem, Diamond and boss materials — never by adding merge steps, which would break the one rule.

04

Built to be data-driven

Every merge is one flat row — Category, Output, Input A, Input B, Tier. The crafting DB keys on the unordered pair {A,B} so merge order never matters, and the whole tree can be rebalanced from a spreadsheet rather than from code.

Post-Mortem

What worked

  • The merge mechanic was fast to implement and fast to author for — every craft is one flat recipe row, so adding content never meant touching engine code.
  • It scales without limit. New items cost imagination, not engineering — the tree can grow indefinitely off a single rule.
  • One idea carried the whole economy: easy to learn in seconds, deep enough to build the entire item ladder on top of.

What I'd change

  • Without the two-item merge constraint, I'd allow recipes that combine more than two components — which is actually where the project started before the rule was locked in.
  • Lean into expensive, multi-component recipes. They reward exploration and automation, and that depth is the part of crafting I most enjoy designing.
  • Add the systems that were planned but cut for scope: machines and workstations, plus more storage options like backpacks.

What I learned

Scope discipline, and how to work around burnout. The design I wanted was massive — I wanted to build everything — but the version that shipped was the version I could finish without burning out. Learning to size the project to the energy I actually had, and to protect that energy, mattered more than any single mechanic in the game.
