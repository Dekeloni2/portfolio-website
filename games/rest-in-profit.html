Rest in Profit

A cozy-macabre mobile game inspired by Disco Zoo's digging loop. You're an aspiring necromancer: dig up undead minions from graveyards, deploy them in a dungeon, and let them defend against looting "heroes" while they earn you coins. A collection-and-management game with a dungeon-defense layer, connected through one shared economy.

Design Pillars
01

Cozy-Macabre

Warm and charming about death — a friendly-Grim-Reaper tone, not grim horror and not a joke. The mood drives the art, the tutorial voice, and every bit of copy.

02

An Underused Hook

Disco Zoo's tap-to-reveal dig loop is one of mobile's most addictive mechanics — and strangely underexplored. The whole game is built to give that hook a world worth digging in.

03

Two Loops, One Economy

Digging and defending must each stay worth doing for the whole game. If either one turns into a chore that only feeds the other, the design has failed.

04

Systems as the Star

Monster roles, a trade-off relic loadout, two-lane progression, and an economy tuned to reward showing up, not just waiting. It's designed to be a systems-design showcase as much as a game.

How I Designed It

The 60-second version — the reasoning behind Rest in Profit, from the core problem to what's next. The full breakdown is below; this is the map.

01 · Problem

Give a collection game real depth

Disco Zoo's dig loop is brilliant but underused, and collection games go flat the moment you've collected everything. Could I fuse that dig hook to a dungeon defense so the two loops feed each other and the game stays deep past "collection complete"?

02 · Design Decision

One economy, layered systems

Coins tie the two loops together. The bigger decision was what sits on top: monster roles, a trade-off relic loadout and two-lane progression, which turn "collect monsters" into "build and tune a dungeon."

03 · Prototype / Test

Prove the hook before building on it

I built the dig loop first and validated it in isolation — ~25 of my own dig runs confirmed the near-miss tension was firing before I let combat depend on it. Fresh playtesters later confirmed it independently: they could see exactly how close they'd come, and the frustration kept them digging.

04 · Result

A working loop — and a real diagnosis

The loop closes end-to-end. When combat felt broken — a maxed unit folding at wave 4 — I diagnosed it as a positioning-AI gap, not a balance problem, and the same team held ~10 waves further. See the diagnosis ↓

05 · What's Next

Content breadth & an endgame

Retest the Werewolf and event-economy fixes, continue tuning the 25-relic pool, give free players real Soul Gem sources, then add prestige (Timeless monsters, Disco-Zoo style) so digging stays meaningful past completion.

Playtesting & Iteration

The most useful design work on this project came from reading a result correctly instead of reacting to it: a diagnosis, a guardrail, a playtest I read as a communication problem, and what the next round of playtesting broke and confirmed.

A "balance" failure that was really a positioning gap

BEFORE · folds at wave 4 AFTER · holds to ~wave 14

A fully-leveled squad was collapsing at wave 4, which looked like a numbers problem. It wasn't — my ranged damage-dealer was standing in the melee getting deleted. The fix was a role-based positioning AI (Frontline / Damage / Support, each with a standoff distance derived from its attack range), not a stat change. Same stats, same monsters — it held about ten waves further. The lesson: diagnose the root cause before you touch the numbers.

Offline is simulated, not multiplied

RISK · drifting into an idle game FIX · play the raid out

The easy version of offline income is time away × a rate. That would quietly have turned Rest in Profit into an idle game, and it isn't one: it's collection and management, where showing up is what moves you forward.

So closed-app waves are played out as numbers at the army's real attack rates, damage, defence and healing, with abilities like Undeath honoured, and one rule kept exactly: nobody taps a corpse while you're away. A strong army comes back to a pile of coins; a weak one earns until it falls. A summary card on return shows what happened.

Three feature requests, one root cause

PLAYTEST · "add tap damage", "offline should pay", "raids need consequences" FIX · teach the loop, change no systems RETEST · easier to understand, more fun

Playtesters asked for tap damage, said earning 0 coins offline felt bad, and wanted raids to have a real consequence. Taken one at a time, that's three new features. Read together, it's one problem: nobody had told them the loop. Default mobile-game habits filled the gaps (tap to fight, earn while idle), and none of them knew a fallen monster could be revived.

So I left the systems alone and built the Necroguide: a persistent tutorial director that runs across both scenes, dims the screen, and advances only on real gameplay events (starting an expedition, digging, finding a monster, killing a hero, equipping a monster or relic), resuming wherever the player left off. Then I ran the same test with fresh players rather than assume it worked. They said the Necroguide made the game easy to understand, and that understanding it made the game more fun.

Tap damage was never added. The need underneath the request, something to do while watching a fight, is answered by optional active moments instead. See the case study ↓

All three came from the same instinct: a surprising result is a question, not a verdict. Find out why before you change anything.

Events are part of the economy, too

PLAYTEST · testers skipped most of the game FIX · one bug, two economy rules

Two leaks surfaced in one session. Looping back to a 5-wave checkpoint re-triggered between-wave events almost every wave, so collections completed far faster than intended. That was a wave-registration bug. I fixed it, and also spaced events to every 5–6 waves so they stay a moment, not a machine.

Separately, testers cleared the Ritual Idol so fast that Soul Gems piled up and they unlocked the Hell Pits, skipping everything before it. The Hell Pits now require owning the Crypt first, and their Soul Gem price rose to 200, dropping the more coins the player has.

The lesson: any reward that repeats is part of the economy, even when it looks like an event.

A frontline that couldn't hold the front

PLAYTEST · strong design, folds too fast FIX · bug fix, HP buff, a level-4 identity

Testers liked the Werewolf's design, but as a Frontline he died almost immediately. Part of that was a bug: Devastating Strike fired backwards, so in practice he had no ability at all.

After fixing it, I raised his HP and gave the ability a level-4 upgrade: casting Devastating Strike grants 100% damage reduction for 5 seconds. That finally sells the fantasy of a frontline assassin, who lands one huge hit and becomes a wall for a moment.

Retest pending: five seconds of full immunity may prove too strong.

What held up

The near-miss reads.
Testers could see exactly how close they'd come to a find. It frustrated them in the intended way, and they kept digging.
Relics drive decisions.
Testers re-planned their loadout before choosing whether to go on an expedition or farm coins.
Two players, two strategies.
One built a stat wall and barely dug; the other min-maxed monsters and relics. Two testers is a small sample, but both paths working is the signal I wanted.

Gate access gently, gate power steeply

A guiding rule for the economy that keeps the game cozy while still having a grind:

Digging = linear cost
Expedition entry rises gently (Disco-Zoo style), so collecting never walls you.
Upgrades = exponential cost
Raw combat power is the real grind and the central coin sink.
Premium = time, not power
Soul Gems buy convenience only — never stats — so it stays fair.

Honest about scope: prestige and later biomes are designed or in progress rather than complete. What's playable today is the full loop: expeditions across multiple areas, nine signature abilities, 25 live relic effects, between-wave events, offline raids and a guided tutorial. Knowing what to build now versus what to leave on paper is part of the job.

Case Study — Making Auto-Combat Active

Auto-combat is valuable because it lets the formation, monster roles and long-term upgrades do the work. But it created a design question: what should a player do when they are present and watching a healthy fight? I did not want the answer to be tap damage. That would undermine the army-building game by making player reflexes more important than the formation they designed.

01 · Problem

Watching is not the same as playing

If every wave resolves without a decision, a dungeon can become a dashboard. The player needs occasional reasons to look up, assess the state of their army and act—without being required to micromanage every attack.

02 · Principle

Short bursts of meaningful attention

Active moments should be optional, readable and quick. They reward being present with a tactical choice; they never turn the game into a tapper or punish a player for letting the army run.

LIVE · GREEN GRAVES

A timing decision inside the fight

Green graves rise during waves. Tapping one raises a temporary undead ally, so the player decides when extra help matters: stabilise a losing frontline, answer a dangerous raid, or hold it for the next threat. The formation remains the source of power; the player chooses the moment to spend an opportunity.

LIVE · TREASURE CHESTS

A reward that creates attention, not chores

Treasure chests create a visible opportunity during a raid. A player who is watching can claim the reward in the moment, while a player who ignores it is not punished—the chest pays out at the wave break. That makes attention feel good without making absence feel bad.

Live system · between-wave events

Make the dungeon feel like a place, not a conveyor belt

Occasional events interrupt the space between waves with a real decision before the next raid begins. They give the player a reason to re-engage at natural pauses, while leaving ordinary waves fast and readable.

Live: The Shadow Merchant — buy one temporary contract for a few waves, or banish him and continue. Live: Ritual Idol — an optional formation DPS challenge with tiered rewards. Guardrail: rare, skippable, short-lived and never permanent power.
Deep Dive

Everything below is the full breakdown — the dig loop, the role-based defense, the economy, and the relic system that ties the build layer together. The summary above is the whole argument in short; go as deep as you want from here.

The Dig Loop — In Motion

The dig site is the piece I built and tuned first. You pay to enter an area (each has its own grid size, buried table and mana budget, and a per-trip price that rises gently) and tap tiles to uncover the connected pattern of a buried monster — Disco Zoo's shape-reveal, reframed as grave-robbing.

Everything present in a dig is telegraphed, rarer monsters occupy fewer tiles (so they're harder to pin down), and the whole thing is tuned so you often come up one tile short — the exact moment the game offers a premium nudge. That near-miss tension is the loop's engine, and I validated it with real play before building anything on top of it.

Running dry is an offer, not an ending: 5 more digs for a Soul Gem, 5 free for an ad, or walk away and see the cells you missed, which makes the near-miss visible. Finds are celebrated in proportion. A captured monster's pieces merge into one creature on a book page, coin caches fly to the counter while the number climbs as they land, and each trip ends on a haul sheet that sends you straight into the next expedition or back to the dungeon.

The Dungeon Defense — Role-Based Auto-Battler

Placed monsters aren't static — they move and fight in real time, and they organize themselves. Each monster carries a role that drives a self-forming formation, so the tactical decision is which roles you bring, not micromanaging positions on a phone screen. Players begin with four deployment slots; collecting more monsters unlocks two additional slots, reaching a six-monster roster. The fight runs for the whole session: it keeps going, and earning, while you're off digging.

Frontline
Wants the front and soaks damage. If it's ever behind the pack, it speeds up until it's back on the wall.
Damage
Hangs back and only advances when its target leaves range — so a ranged unit holds deep, and a melee unit kites just behind the wall.
Support
Holds the middle, between the wall and the damage line.

Heroes walk in from the edge and hold a front line, paying a bounty per kill and a bonus per cleared wave. Once there's nothing left to fight, they turn on the dungeon itself, pushing over gravestones and smashing chests. There's no lose screen: tap a fallen monster to revive it on a timer, or spend a Soul Gem to revive everyone. If the whole army goes down the dungeon is overrun, a soft wall that nudges you back to digging and upgrading rather than a punishment. Checkpoints every five waves can be returned to and farmed.

Hits land with sparks, damage numbers, screen shake and hit-stop, and a deck under the fight shows each monster's health, income and DPS. Nine signature abilities are authored as data with per-level tiers.

Progression & Economy

Two power axes, kept in separate lanes so each stays legible — and an economy tuned so the two coin sinks never starve each other.

Duplicate leveling

  • Dupes level a monster, 1 to 5
  • Each level raises its income
  • Its ability comes online at level 3
  • 18 copies to max a Common, 8 an Epic

Coin upgrades

  • Spend coins on Attack & Health
  • Separate tracks, per monster
  • Linear stat gain, exponential cost
  • Capped — a known power ceiling

The currencies

  • Coins — power & access
  • Mana — the per-dig budget
  • Soul Gems — premium, time only
  • Offline earnings, simulated

Dupes deepen what a monster does (income + identity); coins raise its numbers (combat power). Keeping those lanes apart means a player always knows what each currency is for — and why the next dig or the next upgrade matters.

Relics & the Reliquary

The build layer — and the deepest systems-design work on the project. You collect relics and equip a capped loadout from the pool, so every relic on is a relic you chose over another. Two rules keep the choices meaningful:

Rule 01

Rarity = downside severity

Commons carry a small cost, Rares a sharper trade-off, Epics are clean power, and Legendaries redefine a build. Rarity tells you how spicy the trade is, not just how strong.

Rule 02

Every relic stands alone

A relic has to justify its slot on its own — synergy is a bonus between two independently-good relics, never a prerequisite. And you can equip only one Legendary, so it anchors the build.

Six of the 25 relics, one step up the rarity ladder at a time: a Common with a small cost, a Rare gamble, a clean Epic, and the three build-defining Legendaries. Icons are hand-drawn 32×32 pixel art.

The Reliquary levels up with how many you've found, opening more sockets, and relics drag and drop between the shelf and sockets. All 25 effects are live across digging, economy and combat, giving the Reliquary meaningful trade-offs across both halves of the game.

Monetization & Ads

A free-to-play game has to pay for itself, and how it does that is a design decision like any other. I set one rule first and derived the rest from it: premium buys time and convenience, never power. Everything below follows from that line, and the line is also what I refuse to cross.

The clearest test was interstitial ads. Forced interstitials with a paid removal are a common, proven model, but they fight a cozy game built around choosing when to engage. So every ad here is one the player chooses to watch for a reward, and the game is never worse for saying no.

Rewarded only

No interstitials, no ad the player didn't tap. Each placement either follows a reward (double an expedition haul or an offline raid) or offers a way out of a bad moment (skip a revive timer, refill a dig). Capped per placement and per day.

Ad buffs

Timed boosts — coins, revive speed, an extra grave per wave, starting mana — earned with an ad, not bought. Ad-only on purpose: sell them for premium currency and I'd be selling power through the side door.

Soul Gems buy time

Extra digs, instant revives, collecting a raid's income early, a coin boost. Priced so the cheapest pack is a few dozen small conveniences rather than one big advantage.

Free players earn them

Daily login, collection milestones, first clears, rare finds while digging, and a daily ad. A currency a free player never touches teaches nobody what it's for.

What I won't sell

Stat upgrades, relics, monsters, duplicate levels, Reliquary sockets, formation slots. Those are the game. Selling any of them would collapse the coin economy the whole design rests on.

Balanced at zero

Waves are tuned for a player with no buffs, no gems and no ads watched. Spending moves the ceiling, never the floor — otherwise the free game is quietly broken and the paid one is the real one.

Honest about scope: the currency, its sinks and the store are built; the ad network isn't wired up yet, so rewarded placements grant their reward directly while the design is tested. The economics here are reasoned, not measured — I have no live data, and I'd want retention and conversion numbers before claiming any of it works.

What I Cut

Two features I designed and then didn't build. Deciding what not to make is most of finishing anything, so both are here with the reasoning rather than quietly missing.

Parts & assembly

Originally you dug up pieces — a hand, a skull, a ribcage — and built monsters from them. It fit the necromancer fantasy perfectly, and it fought the dig loop: a grid reveal is satisfying because you uncover one whole shape, and parts turned every find into an inventory entry waiting on three more finds.

Cut in favour of whole monsters. The concept art didn't go to waste — the severed hand and the brain became relic icons.

Parallel farming areas

TEMPTING · more to manage, more income CUT · doubles the economy, splits the fantasy

Several dungeons running at once, each with its own squad earning in parallel — the Sushi Bar Idle shape. It's a proven structure and it would have given monsters past the fourth slot somewhere to go.

I cut it for two reasons. It multiplies the economy: every extra zone is another income curve to balance against one upgrade curve, which was already the hardest work on the project. And it splits the fantasy: the fight is about one line holding under pressure, and spreading that across screens turns it into allocation, closer to the idle game I decided this isn't.

Tools I Built to Iterate Faster

Everything here is mine: the design, the C# systems, the pixel art and the tooling. To iterate on a systems-heavy game alone, I built 22 Unity editor tools: inspectors for monsters, relics and dig areas, content generators that keep existing tuning when re-run, level-curve and offline-time windows, and jump tools to test any wave or event on demand. A build hook also fails any release build still wired to test ads.

Audio: A Scope Call, Not a Craft Goal

One thing here isn't mine, on purpose. Authoring sound myself was burning days on a craft I'm not developing, so I wrote a short brief off the loop (what the dig tap, the reveal, the hit, the death and the revive each need to communicate) and sourced against it. The rule I'd reuse on any solo project: decide early which crafts you're developing and which you just need solved, and put the saved hours back into the first group.
